// 01 — Understanding

What is Phishing?

Phishing is a social engineering attack where adversaries impersonate trusted entities to deceive victims into revealing credentials, financial data, or installing malware.

// 02 — Attack Vectors

Vectors of Phishing

Phishing comes in many forms. Each vector exploits a different communication channel or trust relationship.

Email Phishing

Mass-scale campaigns sent to thousands of targets simultaneously using bulk mailing tools.

Spear Phishing

Targeted campaigns using personalized intelligence about the victim to appear credible.

Whaling

High-value attacks targeting executives, CFOs, and C-suite personnel for maximum payoff.

Vishing

Voice calls using spoofed caller IDs impersonating banks, government agencies, or IT support.

Smishing

SMS-based attacks delivering malicious links disguised as delivery notifications or alerts.

Clone Phishing

Duplicating a legitimate email but swapping links or attachments with malicious variants.

Angler Phishing

Attackers pose as brand support on social media, intercepting public complaints.

QRishing

Embedding malicious URLs inside QR codes placed in physical or digital environments.

// 06 — Defence Tips

How to Protect Yourself

Knowing how phishing works is only half the battle. Here are the most effective defences you can apply right now.

TIP-01

Verify the Sender Domain

Never trust display names. Hover over or click the "From" field to see the actual sending domain. 'micr0soft.com' is not 'microsoft.com'.

TIP-02

Don't Click — Type It Instead

When in doubt, don't click the link. Manually type the official URL into your browser to reach the real destination.

TIP-03

Enable Multi-Factor Authentication

Even if credentials are stolen, MFA prevents unauthorized access by requiring a second verification step.

TIP-04

Inspect URLs Before Clicking

Hover over hyperlinks to preview the real URL in your browser's status bar. Look for misspellings and unusual TLDs like .xyz or .info.

TIP-05

Report Suspicious Emails

Use your email client's "Report Phishing" button or forward to your IT/security team. Your report may protect colleagues.

TIP-06

Keep Software Updated

Phishing often delivers malware exploiting unpatched vulnerabilities. Regular updates eliminate these entry points.

// 07 — Self-Assessment

Email Safety Checklist

Before acting on any email, run through this checklist. Click each item as you verify it.

Sender domain is legitimate

The actual sending domain (not just the display name) matches the official organization.

No artificial urgency or threats

The email doesn't pressure you to act in minutes or threaten negative consequences.

Links point to official domains

Hovering over links reveals the real destination — not a lookalike or shortened URL.

No unexpected attachments

You weren't expecting a file, or the attachment extension is unusual (e.g., .exe, .js, .vbs).

Request verified through another channel

You confirmed the request is legitimate by calling or messaging the sender directly — not via reply.

Salutation uses your real name

The email addresses you by name, not "Dear Customer" or "Valued User".

// 08 — Attack Lifecycle

Phishing Attack Lifecycle

A complete credential harvesting attack unfolds in six distinct stages. Understanding each stage helps you intervene before it's too late.

1

Infrastructure Setup

The attacker registers a lookalike domain, creates a phishing page, and configures mail servers to bypass spam filters.

2

Payload Delivery

A crafted email reaches the target inbox using social engineering to appear legitimate and bypass security gateways.

3

Lure Interaction

The victim reads the email, is psychologically manipulated (fear, urgency), and clicks the malicious link or attachment.

4

Fake Portal Landing

A spoofed login page mimicking a trusted service loads in the victim's browser, collecting entered credentials silently.

5

Credential Exfiltration

Submitted data is streamed to the attacker's C2 server. Victims are often redirected to the real site to avoid suspicion.

6

Account Compromise

The attacker uses stolen credentials to access corporate systems, escalate privileges, or launch further attacks internally.
